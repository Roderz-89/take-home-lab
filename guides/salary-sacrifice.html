Salary sacrifice versus net pay

Updated 14 October 2026 · Tax year 2026/27

Scope: salary sacrifice versus net pay pensions. Relief at source schemes are not modelled.

Last checked September 2026 · Tax year 2026/27 · Rest of UK · £35,000 salary · 5% employee pension · No student loan

Two schemes can both say “5%”. They do not leave the same cash in the month if one is salary sacrifice and one is a net pay arrangement. Sacrifice typically reduces the pay that income tax and National Insurance see. Net pay typically reduces taxable pay only.

Worked example

Jordan earns £35,000 in England and puts 5% (£1,750) into a workplace pension. No student loan. Standard allowance.

A — salary sacrifice

Pay treated as £33,250 for tax and NI.

  • Income tax: 20% × (£33,250 − £12,570) = £4,136.00
  • Employee NI: 8% × (£33,250 − £12,570) = £1,654.40
  • Pension: £1,750.00
  • Take-home: £27,459.60

B — net pay arrangement

Taxable pay £33,250. NI still sees £35,000.

  • Income tax: £4,136.00 (same as A)
  • Employee NI: 8% × (£35,000 − £12,570) = £1,794.40
  • Pension: £1,750.00
  • Take-home: £27,319.60

Same contribution into the pot. Sacrifice leaves £140 a year more in this illustration — the 8% NI on the sacrificed £1,750. That is the mechanism, not a recommendation to change scheme.

A second case — £50,000 and 8%

Same country, still no student loan. Sam earns £50,000 and puts 8% (£4,000) into the pension. After the sacrifice, pay for tax and NI is £46,000, which is still under the NI upper earnings limit of £50,270, so the NI saving is still the main 8% rate rather than the 2% rate that starts above that limit.

  • Salary sacrifice: tax £6,686.00, NI £2,674.40, pension £4,000, take-home £36,639.60
  • Net pay: tax £6,686.00 (same, because both designs reduce taxable pay), NI £2,994.40 (NI still sees £50,000), pension £4,000, take-home £36,319.60

The gap is £320 a year, which is 8% of the £4,000 sacrificed. Tax does not move between the two designs. If there is no pension at all, the same £50,000 illustrates as tax £7,486.00, NI £2,994.40 and take-home £39,519.60 — the pension itself, not the NI quirk, is most of the drop from that figure.

When a student loan is also on

In this engine, salary sacrifice reduces the pay the student loan sees. Net pay does not. Replay Jordan’s £35,000 with 5% pension and Plan 2 (threshold £29,385):

  • Sacrifice: tax £4,136.00, NI £1,654.40, Plan 2 £347.85 (9% of £33,250 − £29,385), pension £1,750, take-home £27,111.75
  • Net pay: tax £4,136.00, NI £1,794.40, Plan 2 £505.35 (9% of £35,000 − £29,385), pension £1,750, take-home £26,814.25

Sacrifice is ahead by £297.50 here: £140 of employee NI plus £157.50 of student loan. That is still an illustration of the mechanism. It is not a reason to switch scheme, and it ignores employer NI savings, which never reach the employee in this model.

What the model leaves out

Relief at source — where the pension contribution comes from pay that has already had tax and NI taken, and the scheme claims basic-rate relief — is a third design and is not in the calculator. An employer also cannot use sacrifice to push pay below the National Minimum Wage or National Living Wage; this page does not test that floor. Bonus sacrifice, annual allowance and the lifetime rules are out of scope.
