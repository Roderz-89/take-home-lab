What’s £50k take-home pay?

Last checked 1 October 2026 · Tax year 2026/27 · England, Wales or Northern Ireland · No pension · No student loan · Standard personal allowance

A £50,000 offer letter is not a £50,000 bank balance. Income tax and employee National Insurance come out first. This page is one worked case for the 2026/27 tax year using the same bands as the Take-Home Lab calculator — so the £ figures here match the on-page engine. It is an illustration only, not personalised tax advice or a payslip. Publisher: Rodway Labs. Contact: hello@takehomelab.co.uk.

Worked example — £50,000 gross

A salary of £50,000.00 with nothing else in the model: standard personal allowance of £12,570, no workplace pension, no student loan.

  • Gross pay: £50,000.00
  • Personal allowance in this run: £12,570.00
  • Taxable pay: £50,000 − £12,570 = £37,430.00
  • Income tax: 20% × £37,430 = £7,486.00
  • Employee National Insurance: 8% × (£50,000 − £12,570) = £2,994.40
  • Illustrated take-home: £39,519.60 a year (£3,293.30 a month · £759.99 a week)

You keep £39,519.60 of £50,000.00 in this illustration. That is not a budget until a payslip agrees.

EXAMPLE £50,000 sits just under the rest-of-UK higher-rate threshold and the NI upper earnings limit (both £50,270 for 2026/27). All of the taxable slice is basic-rate 20%, and all of the NI band is at the main 8% rate. See What’s £45k take-home pay? just below, take-home on £50,270 for the ceiling itself, and What’s £55k take-home pay? / £60,000 once higher-rate tax and 2% NI on the upper slice appear.

How the figures were produced

The numbers above were calculated with the live js/tax.js engine that powers the homepage calculator — same personal allowance, same rest-of-UK income-tax bands, same NI primary threshold (£12,570), upper earnings limit (£50,270), main rate (8%) and upper rate (2%). Rounding is to the nearest penny in the usual way. Real PAYE National Insurance is assessed per pay period; this site models NI on an annual basis for a clear yearly picture. The method is also summarised on the methodology page.

Student loan and pension are optional inputs on the calculator. Turning either on reduces take-home further. A Plan 2 loan, for example, takes 9% of pay above its threshold; a salary-sacrifice pension reduces both taxable pay and NI-able pay. Neither is included in the £39,519.60 figure above so the baseline stays readable. See salary sacrifice versus net pay for how a 5% pension changes the packet on a related example.

What changes the packet from here

Three common forks move the illustration:

  • A pay rise past £50,270 — the next pounds meet higher-rate tax and the lower NI upper rate. What a £5,000 pay rise is worth shows the basic-rate maths before that cliff.
  • Scotland — income tax uses six Scottish bands; NI does not change. Scotland versus the rest of the UK compares the same salary on both stacks.
  • Pension or student loan — both leave less cash in the month even when the headline salary stays £50,000. Run those toggles on the calculator rather than guessing.

What this page does not decide

It does not say £50,000 is “enough”, that you should take a job, or that £39,519.60 is “your” take-home. Tax code, overtime, bonuses, benefits in kind, salary sacrifice and Scottish residence all sit outside this single illustration. Check any offer against a real payslip layout before you treat the monthly figure as a budget.
