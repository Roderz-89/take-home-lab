What’s £25k take-home pay?

Last checked 1 October 2026 · Tax year 2026/27 · England, Wales or Northern Ireland · No pension · No student loan · Standard personal allowance

A £25,000 offer letter is not a £25,000 bank balance. Income tax and employee National Insurance come out first. This page is one worked case for the 2026/27 tax year using the same bands as the Take-Home Lab calculator — so the £ figures here match the on-page engine. It is an illustration only, not personalised tax advice or a payslip. Publisher: Rodway Labs. Contact: hello@takehomelab.co.uk.

Worked example — £25,000 gross

A salary of £25,000.00 with nothing else in the model: standard personal allowance of £12,570, no workplace pension, no student loan.

  • Gross pay: £25,000.00
  • Personal allowance in this run: £12,570.00
  • Taxable pay: £25,000 − £12,570 = £12,430.00
  • Income tax: 20% × £12,430 = £2,486.00
  • Employee National Insurance: 8% × (£25,000 − £12,570) = £994.40
  • Illustrated take-home: £21,519.60 a year (£1,793.30 a month · £413.84 a week)

You keep £21,519.60 of £25,000.00 in this illustration. That is not a budget until a payslip agrees.

EXAMPLE £25,000 sits well inside the rest-of-UK basic-rate band and the NI main rate. All of the taxable slice is taxed at 20%, and all of the NI band (£25,000 − £12,570) is at the main 8% rate. There is no higher-rate tax and no 2% NI upper rate — those appear once pay crosses £50,270. How the figures were produced

The numbers above were calculated with the live js/tax.js engine that powers the homepage calculator — same personal allowance, same rest-of-UK income-tax bands, same NI primary threshold (£12,570), upper earnings limit (£50,270), main rate (8%) and upper rate (2%). Rounding is to the nearest penny in the usual way. Real PAYE National Insurance is assessed per pay period; this site models NI on an annual basis for a clear yearly picture. The method is also summarised on the methodology page.

Student loan and pension are optional inputs on the calculator. Turning either on reduces take-home further. A Plan 5 loan, for example, takes 9% of pay above £25,000 — so on this exact salary the Plan 5 deduction in the model would be zero until pay rises above the threshold. A salary-sacrifice pension reduces both taxable pay and NI-able pay. Neither is included in the £21,519.60 figure above so the baseline stays readable. See salary sacrifice versus net pay for how a 5% pension changes the packet on a related example, and Plan 5 on £35,000 for the loan maths once pay is higher.

What changes the packet from here

Three common forks move the illustration:

  • A pay rise toward £28,000 or £32,000 — still basic-rate and main NI, but each extra pound keeps 72 pence in this model. What a £5,000 pay rise is worth shows the basic-rate maths, and take-home on £28,000 works the next round number in the series.
  • Scotland — income tax uses six Scottish bands; NI does not change. Scotland versus the rest of the UK compares the same salary on both stacks, and £28,000 in Scotland is a nearby Scottish worked case.
  • Pension or student loan — both leave less cash in the month even when the headline salary stays £25,000. Run those toggles on the calculator rather than guessing.

What this page does not decide

It does not say £25,000 is “enough”, that you should take a job, or that £21,519.60 is “your” take-home. Tax code, overtime, bonuses, benefits in kind, salary sacrifice and Scottish residence all sit outside this single illustration. Check any offer against a real payslip layout before you treat the monthly figure as a budget.
