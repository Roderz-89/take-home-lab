Take-home pay on £28,000 — Scotland

Updated 14 October 2026 · Tax year 2026/27 · Scotland vs England, Wales or Northern Ireland · No pension · No student loan

Scope: Scottish taxpayers only, one salary. For the same salary elsewhere see £28,000 in the rest of the UK.

At £28,000 Scottish income tax is lower than the rest of the UK in this model, not the same and not higher. The salary sits entirely inside the Scottish starter and basic bands. It never reaches the 21% intermediate band, which starts at £29,527. Employee National Insurance is the UK charge either way, so it cancels out of the comparison.

Scotland

  • Gross: £28,000.00 · personal allowance £12,570.00 · taxable £15,430.00
  • Starter 19% on £3,967 (£12,571 to £16,537): £753.73
  • Scottish basic 20% on the remaining £11,463 (up to £28,000, still under £29,526): £2,292.60
  • Scottish income tax: £3,046.33
  • Employee National Insurance: 8% × £15,430 = £1,234.40
  • Illustrated take-home: £23,719.27 a year (£1,976.61 a month)

Same salary in England, Wales or Northern Ireland

  • Income tax: 20% × £15,430 = £3,086.00 (one band, no 19% starter)
  • Employee NI: £1,234.40 — identical
  • Illustrated take-home: £23,679.60 a year (£1,973.30 a month)

The gap

Income tax in Scotland minus the rest of the UK: £3,046.33 − £3,086.00 = −£39.67 a year. Take-home is therefore £39.67 higher in Scotland (£23,719.27 versus £23,679.60). The whole £39.67 is 1 percentage point on the £3,967 starter slice. Nothing else differs.

This is the opposite direction from the £45,000 comparison, where Scottish tax is about £396 more, because £45,000 has crossed the Scottish higher rate at £43,663. £28,000 has not crossed anything above 20%.

Second example — £28,000 with a 5% salary-sacrifice pension

Assumptions: 2026/27 · £28,000 gross · 5% salary sacrifice (£1,400 a year) · standard £12,570 allowance · no student loan · no bonus · NI modelled on the year.

Salary sacrifice lowers the pay that income tax and National Insurance are worked out on. Here that pay falls from £28,000 to £26,600, so taxable pay is £14,030. The sacrifice comes off the top slice, which is Scottish basic 20%, so the starter band saving stays the same.

  • Scottish income tax: £753.73 starter + 20% × £10,063 (£2,012.60) = £2,766.33
  • Employee NI: 8% × (£26,600 − £12,570) = £1,122.40
  • Pension contribution: £1,400.00
  • Illustrated take-home: £22,711.27 a year (£1,892.61 a month)

Same set-up in England, Wales or Northern Ireland: income tax £2,806.00, NI £1,122.40, take-home £22,671.60 (£1,889.30 a month). The gap is still £39.67 in Scotland’s favour, or about £3.31 a month.

Compared with no pension, Scottish take-home falls by £1,008.00, yet £1,400 goes into the pension. The other £392.00 is the tax (£280.00) and NI (£112.00) you no longer pay on the sacrificed pay.

Why the gap does not move: the £1,400 sacrifice is taxed at 20% in both Scotland and the rest of the UK, so each saves the same £280.00 of income tax. NI is a UK charge, so the £112.00 NI saving is identical too. The only difference left is the 19% starter slice, which the sacrifice does not reach.

What this means: at £28,000 a 5% sacrifice costs about £84 a month in take-home for £116.67 a month into the pension. It does not change which Scottish bands you are in, so the small Scotland advantage stays. Try your own percentage in the calculator, or read the salary sacrifice guide.
