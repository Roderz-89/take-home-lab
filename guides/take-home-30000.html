What’s £30k take-home pay?

By the Rodway Labs editorial team · Checked 17 October 2026 · Editorial policy

Updated 17 October 2026 · Tax year 2026/27 · England, Wales or Northern Ireland · No pension · No student loan · Standard personal allowance

Scope: England, Wales and Northern Ireland only, one salary. For Scotland see £30,000 in Scotland; for loan detail see the student loan guides.

A £30,000.00 offer letter is not a £30,000.00 bank balance. Income tax and employee National Insurance come out first. £30,000 is a common UK job-ad round number — graduate roles, retail management and many public-sector bands sit near it — so searchers often want a clear packet figure before they open a spreadsheet. This page is one worked case for the 2026/27 tax year using the same bands as the Take-Home Lab calculator — so the £ figures here match the on-page engine. It is an illustration only, not personalised tax advice or a payslip. Publisher: Rodway Labs. Contact: hello@takehomelab.co.uk.

Worked example — £30,000.00 gross

A salary of £30,000.00 with nothing else in the model: standard personal allowance of £12,570, no workplace pension, no student loan.

  • Gross pay: £30,000.00
  • Personal allowance in this run: £12,570.00
  • Taxable pay: £30,000.00 − £12,570 = £17,430.00
  • Income tax: 20% × £17,430 = £3,486.00
  • Employee National Insurance: 8% × (£30,000 − £12,570) = £1,394.40
  • Illustrated take-home: £25,119.60 a year (£2,093.30 a month · £483.07 a week)

You keep £25,119.60 of £30,000.00 in this illustration. That is not a budget until a payslip agrees.

Curious how this changes with a 5% pension contribution?

A 5% salary-sacrifice pension on £30,000 puts £1,500.00 a year into your pension but cuts take-home by only £1,080.00, to £24,039.60 (£2,003.30 a month). Each £1 saved costs you about 72p. See our salary sacrifice guide for how that compares with a net pay scheme.

Moving north of the border?

On Scottish income tax bands, £30,000 leaves £25,154.53 a year (£2,096.21 a month): £34.93 more than here. NI does not change. Compare with £30,000 in Scotland.

EXAMPLE £30,000 sits inside the rest-of-UK basic-rate band and the NI main rate. All of the taxable slice is taxed at 20%, and all of the NI band (£30,000 − £12,570) is at the main 8% rate. There is no higher-rate tax and no 2% NI upper rate — those start once pay crosses £50,270. Compare Take-home on £28,000 and Take-home on £32,000 for nearby round numbers.

How the figures were produced

The numbers above were calculated with the live js/tax.js engine that powers the homepage calculator — same personal allowance, same rest-of-UK income-tax bands, same NI primary threshold (£12,570), upper earnings limit (£50,270), main rate (8%) and upper rate (2%). Rounding is to the nearest penny in the usual way. Real PAYE National Insurance is assessed per pay period; this site models NI on an annual basis for a clear yearly picture. The method is also summarised on the methodology page.

Student loan and pension are optional inputs on the calculator. Turning either on reduces take-home further. A Plan 2 loan, for example, takes 9% of pay above its threshold; a salary-sacrifice pension reduces both taxable pay and NI-able pay. Neither is included in the £25,119.60 figure above so the baseline stays readable. See salary sacrifice versus net pay for how a 5% pension changes the packet on a related example.

What changes the packet from here

Three common forks move the illustration:

  • A pay rise toward £35,000–£40,000 — still basic-rate and main NI until £50,270, but each extra pound keeps less than the headline. What a £5,000 pay rise is worth shows the basic-rate maths, and What’s £35k take-home pay? works the next common search.
  • Scotland — income tax uses six Scottish bands; NI does not change. Scotland versus the rest of the UK compares the same salary on both stacks.
  • Pension or student loan — both leave less cash in the month even when the headline salary stays £30,000.00. Run those toggles on the calculator rather than guessing.

The £30k angle: what a 5% salary-sacrifice pension really costs

A 5% salary sacrifice at £30,000 puts £1,500.00 a year into the pension. Take-home falls from £25,119.60 to £24,039.60, only £1,080.00 less, because the sacrificed pay escapes 20% tax and 8% NI. Each £1 in the pension costs 72p of take-home. More on salary sacrifice versus net pay.

Quick answers

What is £30,000 a month after tax? £2,093.30 in England, Wales or Northern Ireland with no pension or loan, and £2,096.21 in Scotland.

What hourly rate is £30,000? About £15.38 an hour gross on 37.5 hours a week for 52 weeks, or £12.88 an hour after tax and NI. See hourly pay to take-home.

How much of a £1,000 rise would I keep? £720.00 at this salary.

What this page does not decide

It does not say £30,000.00 is “enough”, that you should take a job, or that £25,119.60 is “your” take-home. Tax code, overtime, bonuses, benefits in kind, salary sacrifice and Scottish residence all sit outside this single illustration. Check any offer against a real payslip layout before you treat the monthly figure as a budget.
