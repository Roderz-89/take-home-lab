£40,000 after tax in the UK (2026/27)

On a £40,000 salary you take home £32,319.60 a year — £2,693.30 a month or £621.53 a week — in England, Wales or Northern Ireland for 2026/27, with no pension and no student loan.

Updated 14 October 2026 · Tax year 2026/27 · England, Wales or Northern Ireland · No pension · No student loan · Standard personal allowance

How much tax on a £40k salary? £5,486.00 income tax plus £2,194.40 employee National Insurance, so £7,680.40 comes out of the £40,000 before it reaches your account. All of it is basic-rate tax and main-rate NI. The worked lines are below; this is an illustration from the Take-Home Lab calculator engine, not personalised tax advice or a payslip. Worked example — £40,000 gross

A salary of £40,000.00 with nothing else in the model: standard personal allowance of £12,570, no workplace pension, no student loan.

  • Gross pay: £40,000.00
  • Personal allowance in this run: £12,570.00
  • Taxable pay: £40,000 − £12,570 = £27,430.00
  • Income tax: 20% × £27,430 = £5,486.00
  • Employee National Insurance: 8% × (£40,000 − £12,570) = £2,194.40
  • Illustrated take-home: £32,319.60 a year (£2,693.30 a month · £621.53 a week)

You keep £32,319.60 of £40,000.00 in this illustration. That is not a budget until a payslip agrees.

EXAMPLE £40,000 sits comfortably inside the rest-of-UK basic-rate band and the NI main rate. All of the taxable slice is taxed at 20%, and all of the NI band (£40,000 − £12,570) is at the main 8% rate. There is no higher-rate tax and no 2% NI upper rate yet — those start once pay crosses £50,270. How the figures were produced

The numbers above were calculated with the live js/tax.js engine that powers the homepage calculator — same personal allowance, same rest-of-UK income-tax bands, same NI primary threshold (£12,570), upper earnings limit (£50,270), main rate (8%) and upper rate (2%). Rounding is to the nearest penny in the usual way. Real PAYE National Insurance is assessed per pay period; this site models NI on an annual basis for a clear yearly picture. The method is also summarised on the methodology page.

Student loan and pension are optional inputs on the calculator. Turning either on reduces take-home further. A Plan 2 loan, for example, takes 9% of pay above its threshold; a salary-sacrifice pension reduces both taxable pay and NI-able pay. Neither is included in the £32,319.60 figure above so the baseline stays readable. See salary sacrifice versus net pay for how a 5% pension changes the packet on a related example.

What changes the packet from here

Three common forks move the illustration:

  • A pay rise toward £50,000 — still basic-rate and main NI until £50,270, but each extra pound keeps less than the headline. What a £5,000 pay rise is worth shows the basic-rate maths, and What’s £50k take-home pay? works the next round number.
  • Scotland — income tax uses six Scottish bands; NI does not change. Scotland versus the rest of the UK compares the same salary on both stacks.
  • Pension or student loan — both leave less cash in the month even when the headline salary stays £40,000. Run those toggles on the calculator rather than guessing.

What this page does not decide

It does not say £40,000 is “enough”, that you should take a job, or that £32,319.60 is “your” take-home. Tax code, overtime, bonuses, benefits in kind, salary sacrifice and Scottish residence all sit outside this single illustration. Check any offer against a real payslip layout before you treat the monthly figure as a budget.
