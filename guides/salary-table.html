Where the table changes pace

  • Up to £50,270: each extra £1,000 adds £720 a year (20% tax, 8% NI).
  • £50,270 to £100,000: each extra £1,000 adds £580 (40% tax, 2% NI). See £50,270.
  • £100,000 to £125,140: each extra £1,000 adds only £380, because the personal allowance is withdrawn. See £100,000.
  • Above £125,140: each extra £1,000 adds £530 (45% tax, 2% NI).
  • Scotland: slightly more take-home than the rest of the UK below about £33,000 and less above it; the gap widens sharply from £43,663, where the 42% rate starts. See Scotland versus the rest of the UK.

Notes on salaries without their own guide

  • £20,000 to £24,000: a full-time year at the £12.71 National Living Wage (age 21 and over, from April 2026) is £24,784.50 at 37.5 hours a week, so these salaries usually mean part-time hours or the £10.85 rate for 18 to 20 year olds.
  • £28,000 and £32,000: the student loan plan decides the deduction. At £28,000, Plan 1 takes £99 a year, Plan 5 takes £270 and Plans 2 and 4 take nothing. At £32,000, Plan 1 takes £459, Plan 2 £235.35, Plan 5 £630 and Plan 4 nothing.
  • £45,000: still all basic rate, with £5,270 of headroom. A rise to £55,000 keeps £6,537.80 of the extra £10,000.
  • £55,000: sacrificing £4,730 into a pension brings taxable pay back to £50,270 and costs £2,743.40 of take-home.
  • £65,000 to £75,000: if you or your partner claim Child Benefit, the High Income Child Benefit Charge takes back 1% of it for every £200 of income over £60,000. See £60,000 and £80,000.
  • £90,000: the last £10,000 before the taper keeps £5,800; the next £10,000 keeps £3,800.
