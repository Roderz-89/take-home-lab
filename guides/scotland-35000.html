Take-home pay on £35,000 — Scotland

Updated 14 October 2026 · Tax year 2026/27 · Scotland vs England, Wales or Northern Ireland · No pension · No student loan

Scope: Scottish taxpayers only, one salary. For the same salary elsewhere see £35,000 in the rest of the UK.

£35,000 is the first of these three Scottish salary pages where Scotland costs slightly more, and the gap is small. The salary has entered the Scottish intermediate band (21% from £29,527) but it is still a long way short of the Scottish higher rate at £43,663. The rest of the UK is still entirely at 20%.

Scotland

  • Gross £35,000 · allowance £12,570 · taxable £22,430
  • Starter 19% on £3,967: £753.73
  • Scottish basic 20% on £12,989 (£16,538 to £29,526): £2,597.80
  • Intermediate 21% on £5,474 (£29,527 to £35,000): £1,149.54
  • Scottish income tax: £4,501.07
  • Employee NI: 8% × (£35,000 − £12,570) = £1,794.40
  • Illustrated take-home: £28,704.53 a year (£2,392.04 a month)

Same salary in England, Wales or Northern Ireland

  • Income tax: 20% × £22,430 = £4,486.00
  • Employee NI: £1,794.40 — identical
  • Illustrated take-home: £28,719.60 a year (£2,393.30 a month)

The gap

Income tax in Scotland minus the rest of the UK: £4,501.07 − £4,486.00 = £15.07 a year. Scottish take-home is £15.07 lower (£28,704.53 versus £28,719.60). Two 1-point effects nearly cancel. The starter band saves 1 point on £3,967, which is £39.67. The intermediate band costs an extra 1 point on £5,474 versus the rest-of-UK 20%, which is £54.74. Net extra tax is £54.74 − £39.67 = £15.07.

That is not the £45,000 story. On the Scotland versus rest of UK page, £45,000 already has £1,338 in the Scottish 42% band, and the tax gap is about £396 (£396.05 in the engine). £35,000 has none of that 42% slice. It is also the opposite of £28,000, where Scotland is £39.67 cheaper because the intermediate band has not started.

Second example — £35,000 with a 5% salary-sacrifice pension

Assumptions: 2026/27 · £35,000 gross · 5% salary sacrifice (£1,750 a year) · standard £12,570 allowance · no student loan · no bonus · NI modelled on the year.

A 5% sacrifice brings pay for tax and NI down to £33,250. All of the £1,750 comes out of the 21% intermediate slice, which shrinks from £5,474 to £3,724. That is enough to flip the comparison with the rest of the UK.

  • Scottish income tax: £753.73 starter + £2,597.80 basic + 21% × £3,724 (£782.04) = £4,133.57
  • Employee NI: 8% × (£33,250 − £12,570) = £1,654.40
  • Pension contribution: £1,750.00
  • Illustrated take-home: £27,462.03 a year (£2,288.50 a month)

Same set-up in England, Wales or Northern Ireland: income tax £4,136.00, NI £1,654.40, take-home £27,459.60 (£2,288.30 a month). Scotland is now £2.43 a year better off, not £15.07 worse off. The intermediate slice (£3,724) is now smaller than the starter slice (£3,967), so the 1-point extra charge is smaller than the 1-point saving.

Against no pension, Scottish take-home falls by £1,242.50 while £1,750 goes into the pension. The difference, £507.50, is tax (£367.50 at 21%) and NI (£140.00) saved.

What this means: at this salary each sacrificed pound in Scotland saves 21p tax plus 8p NI, slightly more than the 20p plus 8p in the rest of the UK. Either way the Scotland gap is pennies a month. Try other percentages in the calculator or see the salary sacrifice guide.

Your monthly payslip at £35,000 in Scotland

With tax code S1257L and monthly pay, a £35,000 salary shows roughly: gross £2,916.67, Scottish income tax £375.09, NI £149.53, take-home £2,392.04. If your code starts with 1257L and no S, HMRC thinks you live outside Scotland; tell HMRC if your main home is in Scotland, or your tax will be wrong.

Edge cases at this salary

  • A £1,000 rise keeps £710.00 in Scotland (£720.00 elsewhere in the UK).
  • Relief-at-source pensions. Your provider adds 20% relief. At this salary your top rate is 21%, so you can claim the extra 1% on contributions that come out of 21% income, through Self Assessment or by contacting HMRC. For £1,000 of gross contributions that is up to £10.00 a year.
  • Plan 4 student loan. 9% above £33,795 takes £108.45 a year (£9.04 a month). Plan 4 is written off 30 years after you become eligible to repay, or at age 65 for older loans.
  • Moving. If you move to or from Scotland during the tax year, Scottish rates apply for the whole year if you lived in Scotland for longer than anywhere else in the UK.

More questions

Does Scottish tax affect my NI or student loan? No. NI and loan repayments are UK-wide and are based on gross pay, so only the income tax line differs.

Are savings interest and dividends taxed at Scottish rates? No. They use UK rates and allowances, even for Scottish taxpayers.
