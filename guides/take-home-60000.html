£60k take-home pay in the UK (2026/27)

By the Rodway Labs editorial team · Checked 14 October 2026 · Editorial policy

On a £60,000 salary you take home £45,357.40 a year — £3,779.78 a month or £872.26 a week — in England, Wales or Northern Ireland for 2026/27, with no pension and no student loan.

Updated 14 October 2026 · Tax year 2026/27 · England, Wales or Northern Ireland · No pension · No student loan · Standard personal allowance

That is after £11,432.00 income tax and £3,210.60 employee National Insurance (£14,642.60 in total). At £60,000 the top £9,730 is taxed at the 40% higher rate and NI drops to 2% on the same slice above £50,270. Worked example — £60,000 gross

A salary of £60,000.00 with nothing else in the model: standard personal allowance of £12,570, no workplace pension, no student loan.

  • Gross pay: £60,000.00
  • Personal allowance in this run: £12,570.00
  • Taxable pay: £60,000 − £12,570 = £47,430.00
  • Income tax: 20% × £37,700 = £7,540.00, plus 40% × £9,730 = £3,892.00 → £11,432.00
  • Employee National Insurance: 8% × (£50,270 − £12,570) + 2% × (£60,000 − £50,270) = £3,210.60
  • Illustrated take-home: £45,357.40 a year (£3,779.78 a month · £872.26 a week)

You keep £45,357.40 of £60,000.00 in this illustration. That is not a budget until a payslip agrees.

EXAMPLE £60,000 sits past the rest-of-UK higher-rate threshold and the NI upper earnings limit (both £50,270 for 2026/27). The first £37,700 of taxable pay is basic-rate 20%; the remaining £9,730 is higher-rate 40%. NI is 8% up to £50,270 and 2% on the £9,730 above that. How the figures were produced

The numbers above were calculated with the live js/tax.js engine that powers the homepage calculator — same personal allowance, same rest-of-UK income-tax bands, same NI primary threshold (£12,570), upper earnings limit (£50,270), main rate (8%) and upper rate (2%). Rounding is to the nearest penny in the usual way. Real PAYE National Insurance is assessed per pay period; this site models NI on an annual basis for a clear yearly picture. The method is also summarised on the methodology page.

Student loan and pension are optional inputs on the calculator. Turning either on reduces take-home further. A Plan 2 loan, for example, takes 9% of pay above its threshold; a salary-sacrifice pension reduces both taxable pay and NI-able pay — and can pull some pay back under the higher-rate line. Neither is included in the £45,357.40 figure above so the baseline stays readable. See salary sacrifice versus net pay for how a 5% pension changes the packet on a related example.

What changes the packet from here

Three common forks move the illustration:

  • A pay rise further above £50,270 — each extra pound meets 40% income tax and 2% NI (42% combined) until the additional-rate band. What a £5,000 pay rise is worth shows the basic-rate maths before that cliff; this £60k page is the worked case after it.
  • Scotland — income tax uses six Scottish bands; NI does not change. Scotland versus the rest of the UK compares the same salary on both stacks, and £55,000 in Scotland is a nearby Scottish higher-rate example.
  • Pension or student loan — both leave less cash in the month even when the headline salary stays £60,000. Run those toggles on the calculator rather than guessing.

The £60k angle: the Child Benefit charge starts here

From £60,000 of adjusted net income, the High Income Child Benefit Charge claws back 1% of Child Benefit for every £200 of income, and it takes back all of it at £80,000. The calculator does not model it. On top of the £45,357.40 take-home here, a parent claiming Child Benefit just above £60,000 pays the charge through Self Assessment. Salary sacrifice reduces adjusted net income, so it can reduce the charge as well as the 40% tax. Details: GOV.UK High Income Child Benefit Charge.

Quick answers

What is £60,000 a month after tax? £3,779.78 in England, Wales or Northern Ireland with no pension or loan, and £3,633.95 in Scotland.

What hourly rate is £60,000? About £30.77 an hour gross on 37.5 hours a week for 52 weeks, or £23.26 an hour after tax and NI. See hourly pay to take-home.

What this page does not decide

It does not say £60,000 is “enough”, that you should take a job, or that £45,357.40 is “your” take-home. Tax code, overtime, bonuses, benefits in kind, salary sacrifice and Scottish residence all sit outside this single illustration. Check any offer against a real payslip layout before you treat the monthly figure as a budget.
