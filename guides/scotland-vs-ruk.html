Scotland versus the rest of the UK on the same salary

Updated for the 2026/27 tax year · Checked 14 October 2026 By the Rodway Labs editorial team · Editorial policy

Updated 14 October 2026 · Tax year 2026/27

Scope: the income tax difference between Scotland and the rest of the UK across salaries. Single-salary Scottish guides cover each figure in detail.

Last checked September 2026 · Tax year 2026/27 · £45,000 · No pension · No student loan

National Insurance is a UK employee charge. Income tax on wages is not. Scotland sets six bands. England, Wales and Northern Ireland use three main rates. A headline salary can therefore produce two take-home figures before anything else changes.

Worked example — £45,000

Sam has a standard personal allowance of £12,570 and no other deductions in the model.

Rest of the UK

Taxable pay £32,430, all inside the basic-rate band.

  • Income tax at 20% = £6,486.00
  • Employee NI 8% × (£45,000 − £12,570) = £2,594.40
  • Take-home: £35,919.60

Scotland

The same taxable pay is sliced across starter 19%, basic 20%, intermediate 21% and a slice of higher 42% — because £45,000 sits above the Scottish higher-rate threshold at £43,662.

  • Starter: 19% × £3,967 (£12,571 to £16,537) = £753.73
  • Basic: 20% × £12,989 (£16,538 to £29,526) = £2,597.80
  • Intermediate: 21% × £14,136 (£29,527 to £43,662) = £2,968.56
  • Higher: 42% × £1,338 (£43,663 to £45,000) = £561.96
  • Income tax total = £6,882.05
  • Employee NI is the same £2,594.40
  • Take-home = £35,523.55

On this salary the Scottish income-tax stack costs about £396 more in the year (£6,882.05 versus £6,486.00, a difference of £396.05). Take-home is £35,523.55 in Scotland and £35,919.60 in the rest of the UK. The gap appears because £45,000 has already crossed the Scottish higher rate at £43,663 while the rest of the UK is still at 20% until £50,270. Do not treat everything from £29,527 to £45,000 as intermediate — that band ends at £43,662. National Insurance is £2,594.40 in both columns, so none of the £396.05 is NI.

The gap is not the same at every salary

The three dedicated Scotland pages use this same engine. They do not copy the £45,000 gap.

  • £28,000: Scottish tax £3,046.33, rest of UK £3,086.00. Scotland is £39.67 cheaper. The salary is only in the 19% starter band and the 20% basic band. The 21% band has not started.
  • £35,000: Scottish tax £4,501.07, rest of UK £4,486.00. Scotland costs £15.07 more. The starter band saves £39.67 and the 21% intermediate band on £5,474 costs £54.74. They nearly cancel. There is still no 42% slice.
  • £55,000: Scottish tax £11,082.05, rest of UK £9,432.00. Scotland costs £1,650.05 more. £11,338 is taxed at 42% in Scotland. In the rest of the UK only £4,730 is taxed at 40%, and the rest of the taxable pay is still at 20% up to £50,270. NI is £3,110.60 on both sides.

Read the sign before you quote a single “Scotland costs more” line. Under about £30,000 in this model, Scotland can be slightly cheaper. By £35,000 it has tipped the other way by £15. By the time higher rate is open, the gap is hundreds of pounds, then well over a thousand.

Who pays Scottish income tax

You pay Scottish income tax if your main home is in Scotland. Where you work, or where your employer is based, does not decide it. If you move during the tax year, you are a Scottish taxpayer for the whole year if you lived in Scotland for longer than anywhere else in the UK. Scottish taxpayers have tax codes starting with S, such as S1257L. Check yours on your payslip or GOV.UK.

What is the same in Scotland

  • National Insurance: same 8% and 2% rates and thresholds across the UK.
  • Personal allowance: £12,570, with the same taper above £100,000.
  • Savings and dividend income: taxed at UK rates, not Scottish rates.
  • Student loans: same thresholds. Most Scottish-domiciled graduates are on Plan 4 (£33,795).

Wales has its own Welsh rates of income tax (codes starting with C), but they currently match England and Northern Ireland, so this site treats Wales as the rest of the UK.

Worked example — £40,000

Scottish income tax at £40,000 is £5,551.07 against £5,486.00: a £65.07 difference. The 19% starter band saves £39.67; the 21% intermediate band costs 1% more than 20% on £10,474 (£104.74). See £40,000 in Scotland.

Pension relief and Gift Aid in Scotland

Relief-at-source pensions add 20% automatically. Scottish taxpayers paying 21% or more can claim the difference through Self Assessment or by contacting HMRC; starter-rate taxpayers still get the full 20%. Gift Aid works the same way. Salary sacrifice and net pay schemes give relief at your Scottish rate automatically.

Frequently asked questions

I work in Scotland but live in England. Which rates?

Rest-of-UK rates: it depends on where you live.

Where do the two systems cross?

Scottish take-home is higher below roughly £33,000 and lower above it, with the gap widening sharply above £43,663, where the 42% rate begins.

Is National Insurance different in Scotland?

No. Employee National Insurance uses the same UK thresholds and rates everywhere: 8% on earnings between £12,570 and £50,270, then 2% above. Only income tax on earnings uses Scottish bands.

Do student loans work differently in Scotland?

Most people who studied in Scotland repay Plan 4, at 9% of pay above £33,795 a year. Which plan you repay depends on where and when you took out the loan, not on whether you pay Scottish income tax. See the Plan 4 guide.
